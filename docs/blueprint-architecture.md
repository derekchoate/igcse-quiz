# The Blueprint Architecture — Recognition Across All Subjects
### How the learner builds, tests, and owns his own library of problem-patterns

Engine-level document, alongside `foundations-diagnostic-layer.md`. Applies to every course. Supersedes the "six blueprints" framing inside the maths plan's Strand W (which now points here).

---

## Part 1 — The principle

Expertise research is consistent: what separates experts is not superior reasoning but a large library of **recognised situations with attached responses**. Exams reward exactly this — nearly every IGCSE question is an instance of a recurring pattern. This architecture builds that library deliberately.

**The constraint that shapes everything: the library is never issued, always minted.** The learner rejects memorisation, and he is right to — a taxonomy handed down is a list to memorise, and it would die the death all lists die with him. Instead, blueprints are created **by him, at the moment of understanding, as a by-product of learning he was already doing**. This is the Rebuilder philosophy extended: a formula is a souvenir of a derivation; a blueprint is a souvenir of a solved problem-type. The evidence is on our side here too — the generation effect (self-produced material is retained far better than presented material) and the self-explanation literature both predict that a minted blueprint outperforms an issued one.

A blueprint, uniformly across subjects, is: **a name he chose · gaps to fill · the rule written as a sentence · tells (how to recognise it in the wild) · a link back to the moment it was minted** (the exhibit, discovery, or worked example — its Rebuild button). Nothing on it needs remembering; everything on it can be reconstructed.

## Part 2 — The minting mechanic (universal)

At the natural end of a discovery where a repeatable pattern has just been used — a formula derived, a question-type conquered, a form's conventions assembled — the module offers, never demands:

> "You've just done something repeatable. Want to keep the blueprint?"

Minting is a short bench activity (60–90 seconds), scaffolded from **his own just-completed work**, not from blank fields:
1. The worked example he just did sits on the bench.
2. He drags the specific values out, leaving **gaps** — literally hollowing his own example into a template.
3. He writes (or assembles from his own words) the **rule sentence** — "total = how many × how big of each".
4. He names it. His name, even a silly one, is the name his library uses forever (the canonical name appears beneath as "also known as", for exam-paper recognition — see Part 4).
5. He notes the **tells**: "I'll know it's this one when…" — drawn from what actually tipped him off just now.

The blueprint enters his library glowing, stamped with where and when it was minted. Skipping the mint costs nothing; the offer recurs next time the pattern appears (the Foundations Layer's leaning pattern — offers, capped, never nagging).

## Part 3 — The coaching loop (how minted blueprints become *correct* blueprints)

Self-built blueprints will sometimes be malformed — too narrow, too broad, tells that overfit the example. The correction mechanism is never "here's the right one"; it is **stress-testing**, which keeps him the author throughout:

- **The stress test (in-module):** shortly after minting, and again at Revisit intervals, the module offers a problem chosen to *break* a common malformation: "Does your blueprint survive this one?" A blueprint that holds earns a small glint; one that bends is a discovery, not a failure — "interesting: your tells said Compare, but the gaps wouldn't fill. What would you change?" He edits his own blueprint. (Hypercorrection effect: errors discovered in one's own confident structure are among the most durably corrected things in all of memory research — the stress test is engineered to produce exactly those moments, warmly.)
- **The inconsistent-language collision (maths flagship):** "Tom has 7 more than Ana; Tom is 19" — *more* appearing in a subtraction problem. Designed collision, per the standing pattern; every subject's catalogue marks which stress tests are mandatory.
- **Derek's coaching (the human layer):** the weather map gains a **library view**: every blueprint, its mint date, its stress-test history, his current wording. Malformed blueprints that survive their in-module stress tests become session material — the tutor's version of the stress test is a conversation ("show me your blueprint for X — would it handle this?"). The canonical catalogue (Part 5) is **tutor-facing and Claude-Code-facing only**: it defines what "correct" converges toward, and it is never displayed to him as a list.
- **Convergence, not conformity:** a blueprint is "correct" when its gaps, rule, and tells match the canonical one's *behaviour* — his names and phrasings are never corrected, only its function. Two libraries could look entirely different and both be right.

## Part 4 — The library (the artefact)

One library across all subjects, in the house style — the same shelf holds "THE PRODUCT", "C = 2πr" (minted at the unrolling), "the impressions question" (minted in the English codebook), and "trace it before you trust it" (minted at CS Module 20). Each entry: his name · canonical alias in small type · rule sentence · gaps · tells · mint-stamp · Rebuild link · stress-test glints. Searchable by tell ("it says *per*…"). The library is the single revision resource he will ever need, and he built every entry — it is the anti-memorisation answer to the question "how will he hold hundreds of patterns?": he won't hold them; he'll *own* them, and rebuild any that fade.

MATCH (in any routine, any subject) is then defined as: **search your library for the blueprint whose gaps your facts can fill.** The maths routine's step 4 reads exactly that way now.

## Part 5 — Canonical catalogues (tutor/build-facing; never issued to the learner)

These define the target library per subject: what should exist by exam day, each with its intended mint-moment. Claude Code uses this to place minting offers and stress tests; Derek uses it to spot gaps in the library view.

### Maths (0580 Core)
**Three tiers: word-problem blueprints, formula blueprints, and — per the full 2020–2025 audit — skill blueprints** (command + object → procedure; the audit showed direct-command questions carry roughly two-thirds of Core marks). The adjudicated extension lives in `catalogue-amendments-adjudicated.md` and is part of this catalogue: THE RESHAPE family (collect/expand/factorise/solve/rearrange/index laws, minted A2–A5 and A9), THE COUNT LIST (N1.5), THE READING ORDER (N2.5), THE CLOCK & THE RULER (N11/N12), representation procedures (N6/N9/N11), THE MOVING SHAPE (G16), data handling beyond the mean (D1/D2/D3, incl. stem-and-leaf), ANGLE CHASING (G2/G4, reason-naming built in), constructions & bearings (G13/G12), GRAPHS AS PICTURES OF EQUATIONS (A10/A11), THE GROWING PATTERN (A7), THE TWO-SET SORT (N13), vectors (G17), similar/congruent (G14), circle angle facts (G15), SYMMETRY (G1), NAME IT (geometry-wide), CALCULATOR CRAFT (Paper 3), THE REFEREE (W3).

**The two great arithmetic blueprints** (all one-step word problems are these in disguise):
- **THE SUM** — whole = one part + the other part. Disguises: putting-together (Combine), before-and-after (Change), more-than/less-than (Compare). Mint: W1. Mandatory stress test: inconsistent language ("more" needing subtraction).
- **THE PRODUCT** — total = how many × how big of each. Disguises: bundles (Groups), per-one (Rate), resizing (Stretch). Mint: W2. Mandatory stress test: finding a *part* (division) when the total is given.

**Formula blueprints** — every Museum exhibit mints one: perimeter/area set (G5–G6), circle pair (G7–G8), volume set (G9), Pythagoras (G10), the three ratios (G11), speed-distance-time (N12), density-style rates, percentage multiplier (N7), mean-as-levelling (D2), probability-as-fraction-of-ways (D4). The Museum Guide and the library are the same artefact from here on.

**Process blueprints** (multi-step patterns common in 0580 papers; minted in W3 and late-strand modules): percentage change then reverse ("find the original price"); best-buy comparison; ratio sharing; exchange-rate round trip; area/perimeter of compound shapes (split-then-SUM); "working backwards" (inverse chains); table-reading then calculating. Mandatory stress test for reverse-percentage: the −20%-then-+20% trap (already N7's centrepiece — the collision and the mint share a moment).

### Computer Science (0478)
**Two tiers: question-pattern blueprints and — per the full 2020–2025 paper-level audit — skill blueprints.** The skill tier was designed for all three subjects and is now applied to CS; the adjudicated audit lives in `claude/catalogue-amendments-cs-adjudicated.md` and is part of this catalogue. Its headline: CS's apparent gap (≈half of audited rows) was never a course-content gap and never a verdicting artifact — it was a single, clean catalogue-tier omission. The seven question-pattern blueprints below are all *higher-order* (trace, convert, explain-a-mechanism, compare, kit, build-a-circuit, construct-an-algorithm); the **direct-recall command family** (State/Define/Name/Give/Identify/Tick) and the **structured-recall formats** (tick/classify/complete-table/match/gap-fill) — together ≈87% of gap marks — had no library entry, even though every topic they touch is already taught in an existing module. The fix is minting the missing tier, not building modules. **After it, the honest CS course-content gap is essentially nil:** one dot-point (abstraction) named in M7, and nothing else.

**Question-pattern blueprints**, minted in the modules where each skill is conquered: **trace it** (M20 — the detective's table IS the mint); **convert it** (M1–M3: any base↔base, gaps = value/target base); **explain the mechanism** (the 6-marker shape: component → what it does → why that matters; minted first at M13's packets, stress-tested across topics — this is CS's single highest-value blueprint; **its tells also name *describe-the-process*** — how serial transmission sends data, how text encodes to binary, a logical shift, the steps of a phishing attack — **and *describe-the-structure*** — a packet's sections, an array's name/type/sample/use — as covered instances, so those thinner describe-led rows file here rather than minting a near-duplicate); **compare the pair** (compiler/interpreter, RAM/ROM, MAC/IP — gaps: both sides × the axis of difference); **kit the scenario** (M18/M35: system → what it must know → sensor → rule → actuator); **build the circuit from the sentence** (M31); **construct the algorithm** (input → process → validate → output; minted M22–M25, becomes the Forge's 15-marker spine in the Bridge).

**Skill blueprints** (command + object → the recall and structured-recall the higher-order patterns don't cover; ≈87% of gap marks, every topic already taught in an existing module — so these mint *in place*, no new modules):
- **NAME IT / DEFINE IT** — command + object → the canonical definition or fact in his own words (define encryption, state a robot's characteristics, name the data type, give a primary key's purpose). Minted per module at the moment each term is first understood, **never as a glossary**, identical anti-memorisation handling to maths' NAME IT. Mandatory stress test: **his own wording vs the mark-scheme phrasing** — "does your definition earn the mark as written?" — the one place convergence-to-canonical-wording genuinely matters, handled as translation, not memorisation. Its tells also name the *evaluate advantages/disadvantages of a technology in a scenario* point-and-justify variant (the CS cousin of maths' THE REFEREE and English's attitude questions). Resolves the state/define recall cluster (57% of gap marks) and the characteristics-recall residuals.
- **SORT IT / PLACE IT** — recognise the fact, then place it in the shape the question gives you: a box to tick, a column to classify into, a table cell to complete, a term to match, a blank to fill from a word bank. Rule sentence: "recognise the fact, then drop it in the shape the question gives you." Every module's sort/match/drag-pair mechanic already performs this mint; it simply needs the catalogue name so those rows count. Resolves the structured-recall cluster (31% of gap marks).

*(Legacy currency note, for the Bridge:* pre-2023 database past papers test **QBE / query-by-example** grids, which the current 9.1 syllabus replaced with **SQL** — M29 correctly teaches SQL. Those QBE rows are excluded from the honest gap count as out-of-current-scope legacy; when old DB past papers become Bridge material, re-skin QBE-grid questions as SQL before use. This is the CS twin of the maths pre-2025-calculator and £-vs-$ currency notes.)

### English (0500)
Already blueprint-shaped — this architecture retro-names existing artefacts as library entries. The adjudicated audit extension lives in `catalogue-amendments-english-adjudicated.md` and is part of this catalogue; its headline is that English needs no new modules, only the listing and tells fixes below. Entries:
- Every **question convention** in the Q1 codebook (each decoded convention mints as a blueprint whose rule sentence is its translation and whose gaps are its answer shape). The convention set explicitly includes the short-answer Reading types the full audit confirmed Q1 already credits — the resolution of the audit's E2/E3/E4 as tells-hygiene, **not** new blueprints: **own-words meaning** of a word/phrase [text→own-words]; **find-the-word/phrase-that-means** [clue→text — the reverse / cloze synonym-match direction, a named 2027 type, present too in older papers]; **list/give/identify N** points (multi-point retrieval); and **explain-why / give-the-reasons** (causal).
- **Retrieval** (R1: the evidence highlighter — smallest-sufficient evidence; the home for list/give-N and the reverse synonym-match drill).
- **Inference** (R2: the deduction board — clue → rule → conclusion; its tells now name causal *explain why*, not only meaning-inference).
- **claim–quote–because** (R6, including the **writer's-attitude** question — the character's-vs-writer's-feelings split on the two-sided board).
- **the summary filter** (R7: focus → keep/discard → own words).
- **descriptive composition** (F4 the camera + F5 the atmosphere dial — "descriptive writing has no plot to carry it: the camera decides what the reader sees, the atmosphere dial decides how it feels; nothing else needs to happen"). This is the resolution of the audit's **E1**, the biggest mark-weighted English gap (40-mark Paper 2 comps) — a catalogue *listing* omission, not a course gap: the modules already existed and were simply not named here alongside F6/F8/F9.
- the **form containers** (F8: each form's convention set); **the register dial** (F9: audience + purpose → setting); the **story shapes** (F6); **the persona response** (R10 "Borrowed Voices": bullets → harvest per bullet → set the voice → respond, grounding every claim in the text).
- The carrier chart (Q2) is a *collection* of micro-blueprints (action → feeling), minted continuously. English's mint-moments are already built into those modules' mechanics; the only change is that their outputs file into the unified library.

## Part 6 — Integration notes

- **Routine cards** (maths W-strand now; equivalents elsewhere later) reference "your blueprints", never a fixed list. The maths card's step 4: "pick the blueprint from your library whose gaps your facts can fill."
- **Foundations Layer:** a failing MATCH is a signal; the secret passage can lead to the blueprint's mint-moment (its Rebuild link) — same mechanism, new destination type. Warm-ups may include one blueprint stress test.
- **Constellation Revisit:** library entries join the retrieval pool; a "resting" blueprint's visit is a stress test, not a recall quiz.
- **The Bridge:** past-paper work (P-stages) is re-described to him, honestly and happily, as "your library against the wild" — every exam question is an instance of something he owns. X1's phrasebook entries are blueprints. The exam-day marginal habit is: READ, FIND, then *which of mine is this?*

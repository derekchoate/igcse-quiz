A single lit bulb is a bit. Eight of them make a byte — the one genuinely odd step on this ladder, since it's ×8 rather than ×1024. Every rung after that multiplies by exactly 1024, not 1000 — no matter what a phone's spec sheet quietly rounds to. Step through it below and watch the numbers stop looking abstract.

Every rung says how many times bigger it is than the one before. Which rung's badge doesn't say ×1024?
A byte is 8 bits — that's ×8, not ×1024. Every rung after it, though, really is ×1024, world without end.

Width × height gives pixels. Multiply that by the bits each pixel costs, and you have the file's size in bits — divide by 8 for bytes.

Sound works the same multiplication, with different names. How many samples per second (the rate), how many bits each sample gets (the resolution), and how many seconds it plays — multiply all three and you have the file size, before a single note has played.

Same idea as the picture, different ingredients: how many samples happen every second, and how many bits does each one cost?
Samples per second × bits per sample gives you bits for one second. Multiply that by how many seconds play, and you're done.

Not all compression is equal — and "lossless" doesn't mean "uncompressed"; it just means whatever trick was used, you always get the exact original back. "Lossy" throws a little away on purpose, betting you won't notice. Sort five real files by one honest question: if you compressed this, would you accept getting something slightly different back?

Ask: if you compressed this and opened it again, would you accept it looking or sounding even slightly different?
Code and text can't bend even a little — one wrong bit and a program breaks or a word changes. Sound and pictures have a little wiggle room your eyes and ears won't catch.

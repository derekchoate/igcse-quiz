Every attack on a computer system targets the same weak point. And it's usually not the computer.

Malware, brute-force attempts, DDoS floods, hacking, pharming, phishing, social engineering, data interception — eight different-sounding words for people and code trying to reach data that isn't theirs. Nearly all of them share one soft target in common, and it's rarely a line of code: it's whoever's sitting at the keyboard, tired, in a hurry, or simply trusting. Every threat below falls into one of two families. An attack on the wire goes straight at the system — the network, the login, the code — with no conversation involved at all. An attack on the person goes straight at whoever's using the system, through trust, authority or urgency, and doesn't need to break anything technical at all. Phishing sends you a fake letter — a message that has to arrive, and has to talk you into clicking. Pharming rigs the signpost itself — no message needed at all, because the road you'd have taken correctly has been quietly re-pointed, often by corrupting the DNS lookup from Module 32 that turns a domain name into an address.

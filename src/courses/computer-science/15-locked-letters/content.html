That should be impossible. It happens every time you open a shopping website, message someone for the first time, or tap your card at a machine you've never used before. Here's the vocabulary this whole module runs on, stated plainly so nothing has to be guessed later. A message before anything happens to it is the plaintext — "MEET AT THE CANTEEN" below is plaintext. Scramble it and what comes out is ciphertext — unreadable without knowing how it was scrambled. Whatever key you pick, unscrambling only works if you use that exact same number again — that's what "symmetric" means: one key, shared, doing both jobs.

A shared key is only useful once both people actually have it — which means, at some point, that key has to travel from one of you to the other. In the trick above you could just say the number out loud in the same room. Real messaging happens between people who've never met, sometimes on opposite sides of the world, so the key has to go by some route: a message, a call, a courier. Notice something else here too: nothing stopped the message itself from being intercepted, either — a locked message travelling to a friend could be copied in transit just as easily as the key was. Encryption was never about preventing interception. It's about making an intercepted message worthless without the key that unlocks it.

Press the button — the key leaves on the courier untouched, so your friend never suspects anything's wrong. But somewhere in the middle, the snoop got a copy too, and later uses it exactly like your friend did. Nothing about the delivery looked broken — that's exactly the problem: a perfectly successful delivery can still have been secretly copied along the way.
Watch closely for the one line where the key sits next to the snoop, not next to you or your friend.
This is the courier problem in a nutshell: for two people to share one secret key, that key has to move through space at some point — and anything that moves through space can, in principle, be copied by someone in between. Nobody here was careless; the flaw is built into having one shared secret at all.

Here's a completely different way round the courier problem. Instead of sharing one secret key, you make a padlock — the kind that snaps shut without a key, but only opens with one — and hand out as many identical copies of that open padlock as you like. Anyone, including someone you've never met, can take one and lock a message meant only for you. But there is only ever one key that opens it, and that key never leaves your own desk. This is asymmetric encryption: a public key (the padlock — safe to hand out to literally anyone) does the locking, and a private key (the one key, kept by you alone) does the unlocking. They are not the same key, and only one of them ever travels.

Notice the snoop's padlock is identical to yours, and it still doesn't help them. Locking and unlocking are being done by two completely different things here.
It's tempting to think the public key must be the one that unlocks things, since it's the one everybody's handed out and heard of. It's the other way round: the public padlock only ever locks. Only the private key — the one that's never given to anyone — unlocks.

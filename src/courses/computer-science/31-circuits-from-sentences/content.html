Here's how every circuit on this page is built, stated plainly before anything depends on it: the first two switches always meet Gate 1 first. Whatever Gate 1 decides travels down one wire and becomes one of Gate 2's own two inputs, alongside the third switch. Gate 2's answer is the one lamp at the very end — the circuit's single output. That ordering never changes anywhere in this module, so once it's in your fingers here, it's true everywhere below.

It's tempting to picture one big gate that looks at all three switches at once — but Cambridge draws no such gate. Every gate you'll ever meet takes at most two inputs (NOT, back in Module 30, takes exactly one). A third input always means a second gate, chained on, never a single three-input machine. Below, Gate 1 is AND and Gate 2 is OR — so the whole circuit reads (A AND B) OR C. Flip A, B and C in every combination and watch the notebook fill itself in, eight rows, same binary-counting order as Module 30's byte.

Leave A, B and C all off — Gate 1 (AND) sees 0 and 0, gives 0; Gate 2 (OR) sees that 0 alongside C's 0, gives 0. The lamp stays dark, row 000 fills in as 0. Now switch only C on — Gate 1 still gives 0 (A and B are still off), but Gate 2 now sees 0 OR 1, gives 1 — the lamp lights, row 001 fills in as 1. Switch A and B on too, leaving C on — Gate 1 finally gives 1 (both A and B are true), and Gate 2 sees 1 OR 1, gives 1 — row 111. Watch the middle bulb between the two gates as you go: it's Gate 1's entire answer, carried down one wire into Gate 2.

One more direction round the triangle: given a wired circuit, identify the expression it represents. It's tempting to write the brackets in whatever order feels natural on the page — but brackets have to mirror the wiring, not habit. Whatever meets at Gate 1 always gets its own bracket first; only Gate 2's other input joins outside that bracket, never inside it.

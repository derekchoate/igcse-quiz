You send a photo. It gets torn to pieces, the pieces travel different roads, and it still arrives whole.

Every message you've ever sent — a chat, a photo, a voice note — gets sliced into small numbered pieces called packets before it ever leaves your device. Each packet can take a completely different route across the network, arrive in a different order, and the receiving end still puts your message back together perfectly. Tonight you build the shredder that does the slicing, release the pieces onto a map of routes, and find out exactly why none of this ever goes wrong.

Same deal as always: no marks, no timer, no wrong answers, nothing to hand in. Send as many messages as you like — nothing here ever runs out or gets marked wrong. Take a nudge whenever you fancy one. Stars can never be taken away.

Here's one packet, pulled out of a message that got split into three pieces. Every packet, no matter what network it's on, has exactly three parts in this order: a header, a payload, and a trailer. The header is not a whole address book — it only ever carries three things: the destination address, this packet's number, and the sender's own address (so a reply knows where to go). The payload is just this packet's raw slice of the message — on its own it barely means anything. The trailer marks "that's the end of this packet" and carries a check used later to spot damage. Read the three fragments below, then drag or tap each one onto the label it belongs to.

Tap a fragment to begin.

The fragment reading "To: Receiver's phone · Packet 2 of 3 · From: Sender's phone" is the header — destination, packet number, sender's own address, nothing else. The fragment reading "ELLO" — a bare slice of text with no other information attached — is the payload. The fragment reading "End of packet · carries a damage check" is the trailer. Three parts, three jobs, every single packet.
Ask of each fragment: does it say who it's for and where it's from (that's the header), is it just a raw chunk of the message with nothing else attached (that's the payload), or does it mark an ending and a damage check (that's the trailer)?
The header is the only fragment that mentions addresses or a packet number at all — that's how you'll always spot it, in any packet, on any network.

Type a short message below — or leave the example — then shred it into packets. Each packet is numbered, and carries only its own small slice of the message as its payload (notice how meaningless a single slice looks on its own). Release them, and watch: this is packet switching — a router controls the route each packet takes, and different packets really can travel different paths to the same destination, not one fixed road every time. The receiving end sits and waits, then rebuilds your message using the packet numbers, not the order anything arrived in.

Arriving at the receiver, in whatever order they turn up:

Leave the example message "PHOTO INCOMING" as it is and press Shred it — it splits into four packets, each carrying its own slice ("PHOT", "O IN", "COMI", "NG") plus a header saying which number it is. Press Release the packets and watch all four travel their own lane to the receiver. They arrive, drop into the slot matching their packet number, and once slot 4 fills in the full message reads "PHOTO INCOMING" again — rebuilt from four separately-travelling pieces.
Watch the lanes, not just the receiver — notice each packet is drawn taking a different path across the map, not all four riding the same line.
Look at a single payload slice on its own, before it arrives — "COMI" on its own tells you nothing about where it belongs. It's the packet number in the header, not the payload, that puts it back in the right place.

All that slicing, routing and reassembling costs something, and buys something too. Four true statements about packet switching sit below. Tap one, then tap the bin you think it belongs in — Benefit, or Drawback. Nothing here is ever marked wrong, a mismatched pick just bounces back so you can try the other bin.

Tap a statement to begin.

"If one route is blocked or busy, packets can simply go a different way" — that's the network coping with damage or traffic, so it's a Benefit. "The receiving end has to do extra work, waiting for and reordering packets that took different routes" — that's a real cost paid at the far end, so it's a Drawback.
Ask: does this statement describe the network coping well with something (Benefit), or extra work / extra waiting that packet switching introduces (Drawback)?
"No single line has to be reserved for one message the whole time it's sending" is about sharing the network efficiently — that's a Benefit. "Extra hops between routers can add delay" is a genuine cost in time — that's a Drawback.

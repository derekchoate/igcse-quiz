Programmers got tired of reading bulbs. So they invented a shorthand.

You can already read binary — that's Module 1 in your pocket forever. But eight bulbs is a lot of squinting, and real machines deal in millions of them. This module is the professional trick: one symbol that stands for four bulbs at once. It's called hexadecimal, and by the end you'll be mixing colours with it.

Same deal as always: no marks, no timer, no wrong answers, nothing to hand in. Flip switches, follow curiosity, take a nudge whenever you like. Stars can never be taken away.

Below is a byte. Press the shuffle button a few times and try — just for a second each time — to hold the pattern in your head, the way you'd remember a phone number.

as digits00000000

Hard, isn't it? Even people who do this for a living can't sight-read long runs of 1s and 0s. They needed something kinder to human eyes…

The trick starts here: cut the byte down the middle into two groups of four bulbs — each group is called a nibble (genuinely the real word). The half worth more (128, 64, 32, 16) is the high nibble; the half worth less (8, 4, 2, 1) is the low nibble. Four bulbs can only say 0 to 15, and you mastered exactly that board in Module 1.

Set the nibbles so that…

Each nibble is its own little Module 1 board: 8 · 4 · 2 · 1. Ignore the other half completely while you work on one side — that independence is the whole point of the split.

Each nibble says a number from 0 to 15 — sixteen possibilities. Give every one of those a single symbol and a whole nibble collapses into one character. Digits cover 0–9… then we run out, so ten to fifteen borrow the letters A to F.

Now the same byte board — but watch the new readout. Two nibbles, two symbols. A whole byte in two characters.

in hexadecimal00

Make the readout say…

Work one symbol at a time. For 2A: the high nibble must say 2, and A is the symbol for… check the chart above. Then build each nibble like a Module 1 puzzle.
A stands for 10, which is 8 + 2. So 2A is: high nibble 0010, low nibble 1010. Light those and watch the readout agree.

Here's where the shorthand becomes visible. Every colour a screen can show is three bytes — how much red, how much green, how much blue, each from 00 (none) to FF (full blast). Designers write it as six hex symbols with a # in front. You have three byte boards below. Paint with them.

R red

Colours worth mixing…

FF means every bulb in that row lit — you made 255 in Module 1, this is it wearing its work clothes. For yellow, think about which two paints of light add together (it's not the same as mixing paint in a pot — try it and see).

When the set colours are found, keep going — mix something that's yours. The swatch doesn't judge.

One honest thing before the safari: computers don't use hexadecimal. Deep down it's bulbs all the way — hex exists purely for human eyes, because 4D beats 01001101 every single time. Places you'll now spot it:

Every network device's permanent serial number (its MAC address — a later module):
3C:4D:BE:07:F2:A7
Raw memory, as a programmer sees it when hunting a bug:
48 65 6C 6C 6F 20 44 65 72 65 6B

Two symbols from that MAC address are glowing. Prove you can read them — set this byte to match each one:

this byte in hex00
4D: the 4 is easy — high nibble 0100. D is the symbol for 13, and 13 = 8 + 4 + 1. Two nibbles, done. A7 works the same way.

Notice what just happened — again

You now read two number systems that most adults can't. Nobody drilled you; you split a byte in half, borrowed six letters, and mixed colours until it stuck. The MAC address in that panel above was gibberish twenty minutes ago. Now it's just… readable.

Whenever you're curious, the next discovery is binary arithmetic — making the bulbs actually add, with sparks that carry. No rush at all. The stars keep.

An algorithm you can walk through with your finger.

An algorithm is just a plan — a set of steps in order. Written out as sentences it can get slippery, so there's an older, friendlier way to show one: a map made of shapes. Each shape has a fixed job, arrows join them in order, and you follow the path like a token moving across a board. Tonight you'll learn the five shapes, then set a glowing token walking through real charts — watching it loop, branch, and stop — until reading one feels like reading a street sign.

Same deal as always: no marks, no timer, no wrong answers, nothing to hand in. Step the token, feed it numbers, take a nudge whenever you like. Stars can never be taken away.

A flowchart uses exactly five kinds of symbol, and the whole trick is that the shape tells you the job before you've read a single word inside it. Here are all five. Give each one the job it does — a wrong pick just points you back, nothing locks.

Name every shape…

Two of them are about data crossing the edge of the system — one about the algorithm starting or ending, one about a question. The rounded pill sits at the very top and bottom of every chart.
Pill = start/stop. Plain rectangle = a step that does something. Diamond = a yes/no question (it's the only shape with two arrows leaving it). Slanted box = data in or out. Arrow = the order to follow.

Here's a whole algorithm as a map: a number-guessing game where the hidden number is 7. The chart already has three guesses lined up to feed itself — 5, then 9, then 7. Tap the chart and follow the glowing token from shape to shape. Watch what happens when a guess is wrong: the path bends back up and goes round again.

Just keep pressing Step and read the panel underneath. The token can only ever be on one shape at a time — that's the whole idea.
Each time a guess isn't 7, the diamond sends the token down the "no" arrow, out through "try again", and back up to read the next guess. That returning arrow is a loop — one pass through isn't the end.

Exact same map — but now the guesses are yours. Each time the token lands on the "read a guess" shape, you pick the number. Feed it anything that isn't 7 and watch the diamond send you round the loop again; feed it 7 and the path finally reaches Stop. The chart never changes — only your input does, and the input decides the path.

The diamond doesn't get to choose freely — it just checks your guess against 7 and follows the matching arrow. Change the input, change the path.
Want to reach Stop? Feed it 7. Want to see the loop? Feed it anything else first — there's no cost either way.

This chart should tell you whether a number is positive, but two shapes have gone missing — the dashed gaps marked ① and ② below. Use the two pickers to choose a candidate piece for each gap. Choose pieces that let the chart actually run from top to bottom, and it snaps together on its own. Nothing to submit; it just clicks into place when both fit.

Trace what the chart does before the first gap — what does it have in hand?
It's just read a number and hasn't tested it yet. What kind of shape must come next — one that can send the path two different ways?
A diamond — the chart needs to decide something ("is n greater than 0?") before its two arrows make sense. The second gap is what to say when the answer is "no".

Here's a small chart that keeps doubling a number. It starts at 3 and keeps going while the number is under 20. Before you tap anything: what's the last number it prints? Say it to yourself — or jot it in the box, just for you, nothing gets checked — then walk the token and see whether the loop stops where you thought.

The check "is it still under 20?" happens before each doubling. So the last number printed is the first one that fails that check — it can end up bigger than 20.
3 → 6 → 12 → 24. At 24 the question "under 20?" is finally "no", so the loop lets go and prints. Walk it and check.

Notice what just happened — an eighth time

You now read a flowchart the way you read a street map. You learned the five shapes and matched each to its one job, walked a token through a guessing game and watched a wrong answer send it round the loop, drove that same chart yourself and saw your input pick the path, rebuilt a broken chart until it snapped together, and called the ending of a doubling loop before you ever pressed a button. Tracing an algorithm with your finger — that's a skill programmers lean on for the rest of their lives.

Wires are noisy places. One bulb flips in transit and "HELLO" becomes "HALLO".

Every message in Module 13 travelled across real wires, real radio links, real fibre — and every one of those is capable of quietly getting a bit wrong along the way. Computers catch this millions of times a second, using a handful of tricks you're about to build yourself: two ways of sending bits, three ways of talking, a parity bulb that catches damage (and one famous case where it doesn't), a card exchange that gets a message through no matter what, and a barcode that polices its own accuracy.

Same deal as always: no marks, no timer, no wrong answers, nothing to hand in. Send, sort and corrupt things as many times as you like — nothing here ever runs out or gets marked wrong. Take a nudge whenever you fancy one. Stars can never be taken away.

Here are two ways to send a byte. Serial sends its eight bits one after another down a single wire — slower, but there's only ever one wire to keep in sync. Parallel sends all eight bits at once, each on its own wire — much faster, but all eight wires have to agree on timing. Below, the byte for 19 (his age, in the secret language: 00010011) is about to travel down both. Drag the cable-length slider, then press send and watch each lane arrive.

Send once with the slider low, then once with it high, and compare the parallel lane's two results — one of them isn't 19 any more.
This exact problem is why USB ("universal serial bus") replaced the older parallel cables computers used for printers and hard drives — once cables get longer or speeds get higher, keeping one wire in sync beats keeping eight of them in sync.

Three ways data can flow. Simplex only ever goes one direction — like a one-way street. Half-duplex goes both directions, but never at the same time — like a corridor too narrow for two people to pass, so one direction goes, then the other. Full-duplex goes both directions at once — like a phone call, where both people can talk and listen simultaneously. Note that this is about which directions data can flow, not how many physical wires are involved — a single wire can be full-duplex if the electronics allow it. "A radio station broadcasting to your car radio" only ever sends, and your radio only ever receives — that's Simplex. "Two hikers talking on walkie-talkies" can both talk and both listen, but never at the same moment — that's Half-duplex. "A phone call, where both people can talk and listen at the same time" is Full-duplex — both directions, simultaneously.
Ask two questions of each scenario: can data go both ways at all? If yes — can it go both ways at the very same moment, or does it have to take turns?
"A corridor too narrow for two people to pass" and "two hikers on walkie-talkies" both belong in the same bin as each other — turn-taking is the giveaway for Half-duplex, whatever the scenario looks like on the surface.

Here's the convention this wire uses, stated plainly so it never has to be guessed: sender and receiver agree, in advance, that every byte sent will always have an even number of lit bulbs in total. The eight bulbs below are fixed — they're carrying 19 again, already lit. That's only three bulbs lit, an odd number. The ninth bulb, outlined in teal, is a parity bulb, and it's yours to set. Toggle it until the live count below reads even, then send — a gremlin will flip exactly one bulb somewhere in transit, and you'll see whether the receiver notices.

This is a real, interesting limit of the trick, not a mistake you made.
Count the lit bulbs after the gremlin strikes twice — is the total the same kind of number (even or odd) as before it struck?
Even again — so the receiver sees nothing wrong and accepts the byte as sent. Parity only ever catches an odd number of flips; two flips (or four, or any even number) cancel each other out from parity's point of view. That's exactly why real systems also use checksums — a second, different kind of total — to catch some of what a single parity bulb misses.

Catching an error is only half the job — something has to happen next. This is ARQ (automatic repeat request): the sender sends, the receiver checks, and the receiver sends back a small reply of its own — a positive acknowledgement (ACK) if all is well, or a negative one (NAK) asking for a resend if it isn't. And if that reply never turns up at all? The sender keeps a timer running — a timeout — and resends anyway rather than wait forever. Play out all three below.

Play out…

Press "Deliver it cleanly" — the sender sends, the receiver checks and finds nothing wrong, and the receiver sends back an ACK. Done in one round trip. Now press "Damage it in transit" — the receiver's check fails, so it sends back a NAK, and the sender resends until a check finally passes. Now press "Lose the acknowledgement" — the receiver's check passes and it sends an ACK, but that ACK never arrives back; the sender's timer runs out (a timeout) with no reply, so it resends just in case, and the receiver — having already been fine the first time — checks it again and acknowledges once more.
Watch who sends the acknowledgement in every single scenario — it's always the receiver replying back to the sender, never the other way round.
In the third scenario, nothing was actually wrong with the message or the check — only the acknowledgement itself went missing. The timeout is what rescues that situation: no reply within the time limit is itself treated as a reason to resend.

Here's the barcode from a can of soft drink — thirteen digits. Only the first twelve are the actual product number. The thirteenth, outlined in teal, is a check digit: a value worked out from the other twelve using a fixed rule, stuck on the end purely so a scanner can tell if a digit got misread. It isn't part of the number at all — it's a running spot-check on it. Tap any of the twelve number digits to bump it (it cycles 0–9), and watch what the check digit has to say about it. The same idea is used on ISBNs, the check-digit codes printed on every book.

Press "Put it back exactly as printed" to restore it, then try tapping one of the last few digits instead — same result: any single digit changing anywhere in the number throws the check off.
Try an early digit first, then reset and try one nearer the end — the check digit reacts to a change anywhere in the number, not just the ones close to it.
The exact arithmetic behind the check digit doesn't matter here — what matters is the idea: it's calculated from the other twelve digits, and any single digit changing breaks the match. That's the whole trick, on every barcode and every ISBN you've ever scanned.

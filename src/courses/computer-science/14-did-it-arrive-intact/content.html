Wires are noisy places. One bulb flips in transit and "HELLO" becomes "HALLO".

Every message in Module 13 travelled across real wires, real radio links, real fibre — and every one of those is capable of quietly getting a bit wrong along the way. Computers catch this millions of times a second, using a handful of tricks you're about to build yourself: two ways of sending bits, three ways of talking, a parity bulb that catches damage (and one famous case where it doesn't), a card exchange that gets a message through no matter what, and a barcode that polices its own accuracy.

Same deal as always: no marks, no timer, no wrong answers, nothing to hand in. Send, sort and corrupt things as many times as you like — nothing here ever runs out or gets marked wrong. Take a nudge whenever you fancy one. Stars can never be taken away.

Here are two ways to send a byte. Serial sends its eight bits one after another down a single wire — slower, but there's only ever one wire to keep in sync. Parallel sends all eight bits at once, each on its own wire — much faster, but all eight wires have to agree on timing. Below, the byte for 19 (his age, in the secret language: 00010011) is about to travel down both. Each travelling dot carries a small number — its bit position, counted from the right, so the rightmost bit (the 1s column) is position 1 and the leftmost (the 128s column) is position 8. This exact problem is why USB ("universal serial bus") replaced the older parallel cables computers used for printers and hard drives — once cables get longer or speeds get higher, keeping one wire in sync beats keeping eight of them in sync.

Three ways data can flow. Simplex only ever goes one direction — like a one-way street. Half-duplex goes both directions, but never at the same time — like a corridor too narrow for two people to pass, so one direction goes, then the other. Full-duplex goes both directions at once — like a phone call, where both people can talk and listen simultaneously. Note that this is about which directions data can flow, not how many physical wires are involved — a single wire can be full-duplex if the electronics allow it. "Two hikers talking on walkie-talkies" can both talk and both listen, but never at the same moment — that's Half-duplex. "A phone call, where both people can talk and listen at the same time" is Full-duplex — both directions, simultaneously.
Ask two questions of each scenario: can data go both ways at all? If yes — can it go both ways at the very same moment, or does it have to take turns?
"A corridor too narrow for two people to pass" and "two hikers on walkie-talkies" both belong in the same bin as each other — turn-taking is the giveaway for Half-duplex, whatever the scenario looks like on the surface.

But look up at the corner again: a small amber question mark lights up there instead — nothing in the numbers told it to, which is exactly the point. Look at the byte row itself: it's not the same three bulbs lit any more, so the actual value is 35, not 19 — and no green check appears on the ring either, because the byte really is wrong, and nothing here pretends otherwise. Same count, same parity reading, same tick — but a question mark where a flag would have been, and a genuinely different byte. That's parity's real limit: it only ever checks how many bulbs are lit, never which ones.
Count the lit data bulbs after the gremlin strikes twice — is that count the same kind of number (even or odd) as before it struck? Then look at the two marks the send leaves behind: the checkbox next to the count, and the green check on the ring itself — do they agree with each other, or not?
Even again — so the receiver sees nothing wrong and accepts the byte as sent, 35 and all. Parity only ever catches an odd number of flips; two flips (or four, or any even number) cancel each other out from parity's point of view. That's exactly why real systems also use checksums — a second, different kind of total — to catch some of what a single parity bulb misses.

Catching an error is only half the job — something has to happen next. This is ARQ (automatic repeat request): the sender sends, the receiver checks, and the receiver sends back a small reply of its own — a positive acknowledgement (ACK) if all is well, or a negative one (NAK) asking for a resend if it isn't. And if that reply never turns up at all? The sender keeps a timer running — a timeout — and resends anyway rather than wait forever. Play out all three below as a message thread: the sender's own messages sit on the left, the receiver's on the right — the same left/right the wire diagrams above already use.

Press "Deliver it cleanly" — the sender sends, the receiver checks and finds nothing wrong, and the receiver sends back an ACK. Done in one round trip. Now press "Damage it in transit" — the receiver's check fails, so it sends back a NAK, and the sender resends until a check finally passes. Now press "Lose the acknowledgement" — the receiver's check passes and it sends an ACK, but that ACK never arrives back; the sender's timer runs out (a timeout) with no reply, so it resends just in case, and the receiver — having already been fine the first time — checks it again and acknowledges once more.
Watch who sends the acknowledgement in every single scenario — it's always the receiver replying back to the sender, never the other way round.
In the third scenario, nothing was actually wrong with the message or the check — only the acknowledgement itself went missing. The timeout is what rescues that situation: no reply within the time limit is itself treated as a reason to resend.

Here's the barcode from a can of soft drink — thirteen digits. Only the first twelve are the actual product number. The thirteenth, outlined in teal, is a check digit: a value worked out from the other twelve using a fixed rule, stuck on the end purely so a scanner can tell if a digit got misread. It isn't part of the number at all — it's a running spot-check on it. Tap any of the twelve number digits to bump it (it cycles 0–9), and watch what the check digit has to say about it. A fourteenth tile, held a little apart, shows that same rule worked out live from whichever twelve digits are currently on screen — as a digit you can look at directly, not a number you have to read off in a sentence — teal while it matches the printed one, amber the moment it doesn't, with a small amber flag lighting up right beside it the instant the two disagree. The same idea is used on ISBNs, the check-digit codes printed on every book.

The exact arithmetic behind the check digit doesn't matter here — what matters is the idea: it's calculated from the other twelve digits, and any single digit changing breaks the match. That's the whole trick, on every barcode and every ISBN you've ever scanned.

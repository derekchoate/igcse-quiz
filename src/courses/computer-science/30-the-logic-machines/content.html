Underneath every IF, every ADD, every pixel: six tiny machines that only ever answer yes or no.

Every one of those six machines is called a logic gate. Each one takes one or two switches worth of input and lights a single lamp on the other side — nothing fancier than that. Tonight you build all six on a real bench: input switches on the left (the very same bulbs from Module 1, doing a new job), a symbol in the middle, one output lamp on the right. Flip every combination you can and watch the notebook beneath each bench fill itself in as you go — it's a record of what you tried, never a form to hand in.

Same deal as always: no marks, no timer, no wrong answers, nothing to hand in. Every cell in every notebook lights up the moment you actually try that combination — nothing to submit, nothing that can be wrong. Take a nudge whenever you fancy one. Stars can never be taken away.

Cambridge draws NOT as a triangle pointing at its output, with a small circle — called a bubble — sitting right on the tip. That bubble is the entire trick: wherever it appears on any gate, it means "flip whatever arrives." NOT is also the one gate in this whole module with a single input; every other gate you meet tonight takes exactly two, never more, never fewer. Flip the one switch below, labelled A, and watch the notebook fill in both of its rows.

Tick these off as you flip A…

Leave A off — the readout says "A = 0 → output = 1", the lamp is lit, and the notebook's first row fills in: 0 → 1. Now flip A on — the readout flips to "A = 1 → output = 0", the lamp goes dark, and the second row fills in: 1 → 0. Two switch positions, two rows, and the notebook is already complete — that's the whole gate.
There are only two possible things A can be — try both, in either order, and you'll have found everything NOT can ever do.
NOT never looks at anything except true and false — it doesn't care what A represents in the real world. A off gives an output of 1; A on gives an output of 0. That's the entire rule, both rows of it.

Back in Module 11, AND was a narrow double door — both had to open — and OR was a wide one, where either door opening was enough. Here they are as real components, with the symbols Cambridge actually draws. AND is flat along its back edge and rounded at the front, like a capital D lying on its side. OR curves inward along its back edge and comes to a point at the front, like a shield. Both gates take exactly two inputs, A and B, never more. Flip every combination on both benches below and fill in both notebooks.

Tick these off as you complete each notebook…

On the AND bench, switch A on with B still off — the lamp stays dark, because AND needs both switches true at once. Now switch B on too — both are true, and the lamp finally lights. On the OR bench, switch A on with B off — the lamp lights straight away, because OR only needs one true input. Try every combination on both benches until every row of both notebooks has filled itself in.
Each bench has exactly four possible switch combinations — off/off, off/on, on/off, on/on. Try all four on the AND bench, then all four on the OR bench.
AND only lights for one single combination out of the four — both switches on. OR lights for three out of the four — every combination except both switches off. Same two switches, two very different gates, exactly like the double doors in Module 11.

Take AND's symbol and add a bubble to its output, and you get NAND — an AND gate wearing a NOT hat, flipping its answer on the way out. Add that same bubble to OR's symbol and you get NOR. The bubble always means exactly the same single thing wherever it turns up: flip this. NAND isn't a vague "not quite AND" — it's precisely AND's answer, inverted, every single time, with nothing fuzzy about it. Same goes for NOR and OR. Fill in both notebooks below.

Tick these off as you complete each notebook…

On the NAND bench, switch both A and B on — an ordinary AND gate would light here, but NAND's bubble flips it, so the lamp actually goes dark. Switch either one back off — AND would go dark, so NAND's bubble flips that to lit instead. On the NOR bench, leave both switches off — OR would stay dark, so NOR's bubble flips it to lit; switch either one on and NOR goes dark exactly where OR would have lit.
Work out what plain AND would do for a combination, then flip that one answer — that's exactly what NAND does. Same trick for NOR against OR.
NAND lights for every combination except both-on — the one combination that lit plain AND. NOR lights only for both-off — the one combination that left plain OR dark. The bubble never does anything except invert, but it inverts every single row, not just some of them.

XOR shares OR's curved shape, but with one extra curved line drawn just behind its back edge — that second line is the only thing telling XOR and OR apart on paper. XOR answers one very specific question: are these two inputs different? Try the four combinations below and watch when the lamp lights.

Find all four combinations…

Leave both switches off — they agree (both false), so the lamp stays dark. Switch only A on — now they disagree, and the lamp lights. Switch B on too, leaving A on — they agree again (both true this time), and the lamp goes dark, even though both switches are lit. Finally switch A back off, leaving only B on — they disagree once more, and the lamp lights again. It lit for 01 and 10, and stayed dark for 00 and 11 — exactly the "are these different?" rule, nothing more.
Try the four combinations and watch exactly when the lamp lights, not just whether either switch is on.
It lit for 01 and 10, but not for 00 or 11. XOR only cares whether the two inputs disagree — two lit switches leave it just as dark as two unlit ones. Remember the addition machine from Module 3? The sum bulb that lit for exactly one lit input, and stayed dark when both bulbs agreed, was XOR the entire time — and the carry bulb beside it, which only ever lit when both inputs were true, was AND all along.

Every gate you've met tonight takes at most two inputs — Cambridge draws it that way without exception. So how can a three-input truth table exist at all? By chaining gates together. The box below is really two AND gates wired one after another: A AND B first, and that result AND C second. Module 31 shows the wiring in full; tonight, just flip A, B and C in every combination and watch the notebook fill its eight rows. Order matters here: Cambridge always lists the rows counting in binary — 000, 001, 010, 011, 100, 101, 110, 111 — exactly the byte-counting trick from Module 1, just three switches instead of eight. Get that counting pattern into your fingers now, and every truth table you fill in from here on falls into the same order without having to think about it — it's also how Cambridge expects the rows ordered.

Work through all eight rows, in counting order if you like…

Leave A, B and C all off — that's row 000, and both AND gates stay dark, so the final lamp stays dark too: output 0. Switch only C on — that's row 001, still 0, since the first AND gate (A and B) never lit. Now switch A and B on too, leaving C on — that's row 111: the first AND gate lights because A and B are both true, and the second AND gate lights because that result and C are both true. Output 1, and that's the only row where every switch is lit.
Count in binary with three switches, exactly like the very first byte you ever lit in Module 1: 000, then 001, then 010, and so on up to 111. Working through them in that order means you'll never wonder whether you've missed a row.
The final lamp only lights on row 111 — every other row leaves at least one of the two chained AND gates dark, and one dark AND gate anywhere in the chain is enough to keep the final lamp off too.

A currency with no bank, no vault, no country. Kept honest by everyone watching everyone.

A digital currency is money that only ever exists as data — no coins, no notes, nothing physical ever changes hands. Handing someone a banknote proves a trade happened just by existing in their hand afterwards. A digital currency has no such object, so it needs something else to prove the same thing: a record everyone trusts. Tonight you build that record from scratch, break it on purpose, and watch exactly why breaking it turns out to be spectacularly hard to get away with.

A small village fund keeps a shared ledger of IOUs — short for "I owe you", a running note of who owes what to whom. Arun has kept the fund's one and only book since it started. Below are the four entries in it right now. Each has a "quietly change this entry" button next to it — press one and watch what happens.

Nothing's been touched yet.

Press "Quietly change this entry" on "Priya lent Farah 20 credits." — it becomes "Priya lent Farah 80 credits." instantly. No warning appears, no second opinion gets consulted, nothing turns red. Arun's book is the only copy of the truth that exists, so whatever Arun's book says, that's simply what happened now — there's no way for anyone to even notice, let alone object.
Pick any one of the four entries and press its "quietly change this entry" button.
Once you've changed one, ask yourself: is there anything, anywhere, that could catch this? If the answer is no, you've found the trust problem this whole module is about.

One obvious fix: stop trusting a single book. Give everyone their own copy of the exact same ledger, kept independently, and let anyone compare copies whenever they like. Below, Arun, Priya and Wei Ling each hold an identical copy of the same four entries. Try the same trick as before — quietly change one entry on Arun's copy only — then ask everyone to compare.

Press "Quietly change Arun's copy" — his copy now says "Priya lent Farah 80 credits.", while Priya's and Wei Ling's copies still both say 20. Press "Ask everyone to compare copies" and the disagreement is right there: two independent copies agree with each other, and Arun's is the odd one out. Nobody needed a referee — the disagreement itself is the tell. Back in Module 32, packets crossed a network with no single company owning the whole thing; this is the same idea applied to a record instead of a delivery.
Change only Arun's copy — leave the other two exactly as they are — then compare all three.
With three independent copies, a lone tampered one doesn't quietly become the new truth — it just becomes the copy that disagrees with the other two.

Real digital currency systems take "everyone keeps a copy" further, with a structure called a blockchain. Transactions get grouped into blocks, added one after another to form a chain. Every block, except the very first, stores a fingerprint of the block before it — a short code calculated from that block's contents, so any change to those contents produces a completely different fingerprint. Storing that fingerprint is how one block grips the one before it: as long as the stored copy still matches the earlier block's own fingerprint, nothing has moved. Below is the fund's own history, redrawn as a chain of five blocks. Inspect each one and check its grip on the block before it.

Press "Inspect block 2" — its fingerprint is 8D4E-11A0, and it stores a copy of block 1's fingerprint, 3F2A-9B1C, right alongside it. Block 1's own fingerprint (shown on block 1) is also 3F2A-9B1C — they match, so block 2's grip on block 1 is intact. Every block below works exactly the same way, right down the chain.
Inspect block 1 first — notice it has nothing before it to grip, since it's where the chain starts.
For every other block, compare its "stored copy of the previous block's fingerprint" against the fingerprint actually printed on that previous block. Matching is the whole game.

Now do to the chain what you did to Arun's lone book back in the first discovery — except this time, watch what a fingerprint does about it. The chain below is the same five blocks. Sneak into block 3 and change its entry, then compare what happens next.

Press "Sneak into block 3 and change the entry" — its transaction becomes "Wei Ling sends Priya 90 credits." and its fingerprint recalculates to 9E30-77FA. Press "Compare block 4's stored fingerprint with block 3's new one" — block 4 is still holding its original stored copy, C71B-5602, and that no longer matches block 3's new fingerprint at all. Block 4's grip on block 3 has failed — and since block 5 grips block 4 the exact same way, block 5 stops verifying too, without you touching it at all. Meanwhile, the other villagers' copies of the whole chain never changed, so they still show block 3's original entry — they simply disagree with yours.
After your edit, compare block 4's stored fingerprint with block 3's new one.
They no longer match. What would you have to redo, and on how many copies, to make block 4 look like it always agreed with your edit?
Every block after it — on most copies of the chain out there — faster than everyone else adds new, honest blocks on top. That's why tampering fails: not because it's forbidden, but because catching up is a race nobody wins.

Digital currency attracts a lot of talk, and not all of it holds up. Below are six claims — some are straightforward fact, matched to exactly what you've just built; the rest are hype, sanded down from things people say that don't survive contact with how a chain of fingerprints actually behaves. Sort each one onto its pile.

Tap a claim to begin.

"A digital currency only ever exists electronically — there's no coin or note behind it" — that's exactly the definition this module opened with. Fact pile. "Once a block's been added, quietly editing an old transaction rewrites history with nobody able to tell" — you just spent a whole discovery watching the opposite happen: editing block 3 broke its grip on block 4 immediately, and that's precisely what got noticed. A blockchain is the mechanism — the chain of gripped, fingerprinted blocks. A digital currency is one thing that mechanism can track. They're not the same word for the same thing.

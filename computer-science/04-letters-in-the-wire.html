A byte once spelled YOU. Here's the system the whole world agreed on.

Numbers standing for letters isn't new — you did that in Module 1. What's new is that every computer on Earth uses the exact same numbering, agreed decades ago, so a byte sent from one machine means the same letter on any other. It's called a character set, and by the end you'll type on a real keyboard and watch the bytes light themselves — including the oddly satisfying trick that separates a capital letter from its lowercase twin by exactly one bulb.

Flip a few bulbs and you get a number — that part's settled. But a number only becomes a letter if whoever reads it agrees on the same table. Decades ago, different machines used different numbering for letters, and nobody had told them to agree. Below is one byte and two rival codebooks reading it at once.

ASCII isn't a random list — it's a street map. Digits live in one stretch of numbers, capital letters in another, lowercase in a third, with gaps for punctuation in between. Once you know the edges of each neighbourhood, you can predict what kind of character a byte holds before you even look it up.

Three neighbourhoods, three ranges. Digits are the smallest block — try values just under 60.
Digits run 48 to 57, capitals 65 to 90, lowercase 97 to 122. Land anywhere inside a range and that whole neighbourhood counts.

Here's the trick that makes ASCII genuinely elegant: a capital letter and its lowercase twin are exactly one bulb apart — the one worth 32, ringed in teal below. Set any capital, light that single bulb, and watch it become lowercase without touching anything else.

Prove the case bit…

Set any capital letter, then find the one bulb worth 32 — try switching just that one.
A is 65 (01000001). Light the 32-bulb too and the total becomes 97 — which is lowercase a. Same letter, one different bulb.

ASCII has exactly 128 slots — plenty for English letters, digits and punctuation, and not one slot more. Type an ordinary letter below and watch its number sit comfortably inside that range. Then try a character ASCII was never built for, and watch it simply run out of room. That gap is why Unicode exists — same idea, just with far more slots.

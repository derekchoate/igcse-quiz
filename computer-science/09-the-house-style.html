Cambridge has an exact dialect. Tonight you learn to read it.

The algorithms you walked through as maps in the last discovery have a written form too — a tidy, agreed way of putting them into words. It isn't a real programming language; nothing here runs on a computer. It's cleaner than real code, invented so people can share a plan without arguing about punctuation. You'll learn to read it out loud as plain English — and once you can read it, writing it is a short step away.

One symbol does most of the work, and almost everyone meets it and mistakes it for something from maths. We'll clear that up first, before anything leans on it.

Same deal as always: no marks, no timer, no wrong answers, nothing to hand in. Read lines aloud, pair them up, take a nudge whenever you like — every route is a good one. Stars can never be taken away.

Everywhere in this dialect you'll see a left arrow: ←. It does not mean “equals”. It's an instruction: “work out the right-hand side, then drop the answer into the box on the left.” Read it as the word gets.

The trap: in maths, Score = Score + 5 is nonsense — nothing equals itself plus five. Here it isn't a claim that they're equal. It's a command: take Score's current value, add 5, put the result back. The box is allowed to appear on both sides.

Read all three lines to collect the idea…

Read the arrow ← out loud as the word “gets”. Not “equals” — “gets”.
Score ← Score + 5 becomes “Score gets its old self, plus 5.” The right side is worked out using the current value, then the answer replaces what was in the box.
Say Score is 10. Tap Score ← Score × 2: the machine reads the box (10), works out 10 × 2 = 20, then drops 20 back into Score. The old 10 is gone. Tap all three and keep your eye on the box.

The machine doesn't care what you call your boxes — a, x1, banana, all the same to it. But a person reading it cares enormously. Here's an algorithm with names that tell you nothing:

It works out a shopping bill. a is the price of one item (money, so it's a REAL box — decimals allowed). b is how many you buy (a whole count — INTEGER). c is what you pay. Rename the three boxes so the algorithm reads itself.

A good name answers “what's inside this box?” in one word. “Thing” and “n” don't.
Look at the types. a is REAL — it allows decimals, like money. b is INTEGER — whole numbers, like a count. c holds a × b.
a is the price → Price. b is how many → Quantity. c is the two multiplied → Total. Now the line reads Total ← Price × Quantity, and nobody has to guess.

Meaningful names tell you what a box holds. Sometimes you also want to say why — and that's a comment. Anything after two slashes // is a note for a human. The machine reads straight past it. Here's a line that tells you how, but not why:

Anything after // is a note for a human. The machine reads right past it — the answer it prints is exactly the same with or without the note.
Press “Reveal the note” to see what a comment adds, then “Run it” to watch the machine ignore that note and still get the same answer.
t ← p * q tells you how. The comment // total = price × quantity tells you why. Run it: p is 5, q is 3, so t becomes 15 — the note changes nothing for the machine, everything for you.

You can now read Cambridge's pseudocode dialect out loud in plain English. You met the ← arrow and read it as “gets” — sidestepping the one trap that catches almost everyone — watched DECLARE make empty boxes before anything filled them, read a whole snippet line by line and paired every sentence back to its code, turned an unreadable algorithm readable just by renaming its boxes, and saw why a comment is a note the machine ignores but a person treasures. You didn't write any of it — but you can read all of it, which is the harder half.

Whenever you're ready, the next discovery is where you stop reading the dialect and start commanding it — declaring your own boxes, dropping values in, and doing arithmetic with them. Everything you just learned to read, you'll be writing. No rush at all — the stars keep.
